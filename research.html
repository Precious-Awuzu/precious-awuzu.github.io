Predicting hypertension

When I compared six models for my MSc dissertation at the University of Gloucestershire, two produced the same headline figure. A single decision tree and XGBoost each reached 99% accuracy when rounded. That tie changed the question worth asking of the results. If two models score equally well overall, do they miss the same people? The figures below show where my original comparison ended and where a replication could begin.

How the comparison was made

The dissertation describes encoding categorical variables numerically, dividing the data into roughly 80% training and 20% testing, and comparing six classifiers. The chart uses the rounded accuracy figures in the submitted abstract. This is a visualisation of the reported experiment. The training run has not been independently reproduced.

What the comparison tells us

The decision tree matched XGBoost to the reported precision. Accuracy alone cannot show how often either method missed people with hypertension. A replication should reconstruct the target definition, check predictors for overlap with that definition and evaluate sensitivity and specificity on an independent sample before drawing conclusions about clinical use.

What I measured

For my MSc dissertation I compared six classifiers in Python. The reported accuracy counts all correct classifications together. To judge the cost of missed cases, I would report sensitivity for each hypertension class and inspect disagreements between the two highest-scoring models.
